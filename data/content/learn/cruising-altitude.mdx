## Flight levels, not altitudes

Above the **transition altitude** (typically 18,000 ft in the US, 6,000 ft
in much of Europe), all altitude reporting switches to "flight levels" —
nominal altitudes above the 1013.25 hPa pressure datum, written as FLxxx
where xxx is the altitude in hundreds of feet. FL370 means 37,000 feet
pressure-altitude. This convention means every aircraft above the
transition altitude is measuring height against the same reference, so
vertical separation in dense airspace is consistent regardless of local
sea-level pressure.

## Why higher is more efficient

At FL350, the air density is about 32 % of sea level. Drag (the force
that opposes motion) is proportional to density × velocity² × area, so
holding a fixed Mach number at altitude requires roughly one-third of the
thrust required at sea level for the same true airspeed. The engines'
fuel burn falls almost in proportion. The result: every 4,000 ft of climb
roughly cuts cruise fuel burn by 10–15 %.

<Callout type="definition" term="Optimum altitude">
  The altitude at which the lift-to-drag ratio is maximised for a given
  aircraft weight and cruise Mach. Optimum altitude rises as the aircraft
  burns off fuel and gets lighter — a 777 may take off at maximum weight
  and start at FL310, then "step climb" to FL370 and finally FL390 over
  a 12-hour leg.
</Callout>

## Why not higher still

Two limits cap cruise altitude. The **structural service ceiling** is the
altitude beyond which the cabin pressurisation system cannot maintain a
safe cabin altitude — most current jets are certified to 41,000 to 43,000
ft. The **engine flameout margin** falls with altitude — high-altitude
flameouts are harder to relight, and required margins above engine
restart limits constrain real cruise to a band below the absolute
ceiling.

## Cruise altitude affects the contrail problem

Contrail formation depends on relative humidity over ice and the
temperature at cruise altitude. Flights that pass through ice-
supersaturated regions tend to leave persistent contrails; those that
fly slightly above or below avoid them. Recent research (Teoh et al.
2024) shows that 2 % of flights produce 80 % of the contrail warming
effect — and a small altitude adjustment could remove most of that
forcing without significant fuel cost.

<Callout type="note" term="Higher cruise can lower the climate footprint">
  When contrail-aware altitude routing becomes operational, the fuel
  optimum and the climate optimum will diverge slightly: aircraft might
  cruise 1,000–2,000 ft above or below their fuel-optimum altitude on
  certain routes to avoid ice-supersaturation. The fuel penalty is small;
  the contrail-forcing reduction is large.
</Callout>
