## What WGS-84 actually is

WGS-84 is two things at once. First, it is an **ellipsoid of revolution** —
a mathematical surface defined by a and f that approximates mean sea level
to within ~100 metres globally. Second, it is a **reference frame**: a
choice of origin (Earth's centre of mass), an orientation (zero longitude
at IERS Reference Meridian, not Greenwich exactly), and a scale (the SI
metre).

<Callout type="definition" term="Ellipsoid of revolution">
  A three-dimensional shape generated by rotating an ellipse around its
  shorter axis. For WGS-84 the ellipse has semi-major axis a along the
  equatorial plane and semi-minor axis b along the polar axis; revolving it
  produces an oblate spheroid that is the same at every longitude but
  flattens between equator and pole.
</Callout>

## The defining and derived parameters

WGS-84 picks four defining parameters and derives everything else from them.
The defining four are a, 1/f, the Earth's gravitational constant GM, and
the angular velocity ω. The ellipsoid you care about for distance and
position only needs a and 1/f — GM and ω are needed for satellite orbit
mechanics and gravimetry.

## Why these specific numbers

WGS-84's a and 1/f were chosen in 1984 by the U.S. Defense Mapping Agency
(now NGA) to fit a worldwide set of Doppler satellite-tracking observations
collected through the late 1970s. The values closely match the IUGG
GRS-80 ellipsoid that civilian geodesy had adopted four years earlier — the
two share 1/f = 298.257222101 vs 298.257223563, a difference of less than
0.1 mm at Earth scale.

<Callout type="note" term="WGS-84 vs ITRF">
  Position fixes from modern GPS are technically given in ITRF (the
  International Terrestrial Reference Frame), not WGS-84. NGA periodically
  realigns WGS-84 to ITRF — the current alignment WGS-84(G2139), based on
  ITRF2014, is centimetre-accurate against ITRF for static positions. For
  any flight-distance application the WGS-84/ITRF distinction is invisible.
</Callout>

## How GPS depends on WGS-84

The GPS broadcast ephemeris — the per-satellite orbital parameters
transmitted by each satellite to the receiver — is referenced to WGS-84.
The receiver computes satellite positions from those ephemerides, performs
trilateration, and returns coordinates also referenced to WGS-84. A
mismatch between the ellipsoid the satellites use and the ellipsoid the
receiver assumes would inject a systematic position error of several metres.

## The geoid is not the ellipsoid

A common confusion: WGS-84 the ellipsoid is a smooth mathematical surface;
the **geoid** is the irregular surface of equal gravitational potential
that mean sea level approximates. Heights labelled "above sea level"
typically refer to the geoid, while raw GPS heights refer to the
ellipsoid. The difference (the geoid undulation) ranges from −106 m near
the Maldives to +85 m near Iceland.

<Callout type="warning" term="Heights need a model">
  If you take a GPS altitude and want height above sea level, you must
  subtract the geoid undulation at that point — published by NGA as
  EGM2008 (Earth Gravitational Model 2008), a separate spherical-
  harmonic model that pairs with WGS-84. Aviation Flight Management
  Systems do this conversion automatically using onboard geoid grids.
</Callout>
